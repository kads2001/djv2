How Distributors Can Build a Spirits Portfolio That Fits Their Market

For a spirits distributor, portfolio planning is a balance between customer demand, category coverage, working capital, local regulation and dependable replenishment. Adding more products can create opportunities, but a larger catalogue is not automatically a stronger portfolio. The objective is to select products that suit the territory and channels the distributor can actually serve.

Deejay Distilleries’ website lists whisky, vodka, gin, rum, brandy and exotic spirits, with dedicated product pages for several categories. These public listings can help a distributor prepare an initial shortlist. Current availability, territory rights, pack formats, pricing, supply terms and market eligibility must be confirmed directly before a commercial decision.

1. Begin with the territory and channel

A distributor should define the geography it serves and the types of customers it can reach. Retail stores, wholesalers, hospitality accounts and other licensed channels may have different purchasing patterns, product requirements and margins. Local licensing and rules for alcohol sales, promotion, storage and distribution must be understood before the portfolio is expanded.

Map the target customers by channel and region. For each group, estimate the relevant category mix, typical order size, frequency of purchase and seasonal factors. Use actual sales data where available; for a new territory, mark estimates clearly and test them cautiously.

A useful planning question is not simply “Which products can we buy?” but “Which products can we supply reliably to a defined customer group at a sustainable landed cost?”

2. Assess category coverage without overextending

Different spirits categories serve different product and customer needs. A distributor may evaluate whisky, vodka, gin, rum, brandy and other spirits, but should not assume that every category is necessary at launch. Consider customer requests, competitor availability, price positioning, category rules and the distributor’s ability to support each product.

Build a portfolio matrix with the category, candidate products, target channel, expected demand, competing alternatives, required pack sizes and unresolved questions. A simple matrix exposes gaps and reduces decisions driven by catalogue size alone.

When evaluating a product, ask whether it adds meaningful coverage, duplicates an existing option or requires sales effort the business cannot support. A product that appears attractive in isolation may not be a good fit if the channel, pricing or supply assumptions are weak.

3. Evaluate pack sizes and operational fit

Pack size affects shelf fit, customer preference, price point, transport, storage and inventory management. A distributor should confirm which sizes are actually available for the product and legally permitted in the territory. Product pages may show selected formats, but availability and market-specific approvals should be checked with the supplier.

Compare case configuration, units per case, case dimensions, weight, pallet arrangement and storage needs. These factors influence warehouse capacity, picking efficiency, freight cost and breakage risk. Confirm whether mixed orders are possible or whether each product has a separate minimum order quantity.

Do not plan inventory using an assumed pack size or lead time. Obtain current specifications and written confirmation from the supplier before preparing the final assortment and order forecast.

4. Review commercial viability using landed cost

A distributor’s margin depends on more than the supplier’s unit price. Model the full cost to the point of sale, including freight, insurance, duties, taxes, excise, handling, warehousing, financing and local distribution costs where applicable. The exact items depend on the route and market.

Build low, expected and high scenarios for uncertain costs. Calculate gross margin using a consistent basis and distinguish between margin and markup. Include expected promotions, discounts, returns, breakage and slowmoving stock where relevant.

Compare products on a like-for-like basis: same volume, packaging, delivery terms, tax assumptions and quantity. Ask suppliers to explain what is included in the quotation. A transparent model is more useful than a single optimistic margin estimate.

5. Check the supplier’s operational readiness

A supplier relationship needs clear information and a workable order process. Before onboarding a product, ask for the current product specification, approved product details, packaging information, order requirements, indicative lead times and available shipment documentation. Ask how changes in specification, artwork or schedule are communicated.

A distributor can assess supplier responsiveness by providing a clear request and reviewing whether the response addresses the actual questions. Unanswered items should remain visible in the decision record. Do not infer production capacity, delivery reliability or stock availability from a broad company profile.

Create a supplier file for each product with contact details, agreed specification, quotation date, order terms, document checklist and escalation contact. Update it when the product, price or terms change.

6. Plan compliance and responsible distribution

Alcohol is a regulated category. Distributor obligations can include licences, storage and transport rules, tax and excise procedures, product registration, label requirements, restrictions on advertising and promotion, and rules governing which customers may purchase or receive products. These obligations vary by jurisdiction.

Confirm requirements with the relevant authorities and qualified local advisers. The supplier’s documentation may support the process, but it does not automatically satisfy every local obligation. Do not advertise or distribute a product until the necessary approvals and licences have been confirmed.

Build compliance checks into onboarding rather than treating them as a final administrative step. Keep records of approved products, supplier documents, invoices, batches and distribution where required. Train relevant staff on the rules that apply to their roles.

7. Use a demand-led inventory plan

Portfolio expansion can tie up working capital and warehouse space. Set initial stock levels using expected sales, supplier lead time, reorder frequency and the consequences of running out. Use conservative assumptions for unfamiliar products and review actual sales before scaling.

A basic reorder approach compares available stock plus confirmed incoming inventory against expected demand during the replenishment lead time and a suitable safety buffer. The buffer should reflect uncertainty and business priorities; it should not be an arbitrary number applied to every product.

Track sell-through, stock cover, aged inventory, forecast accuracy, returns and customer reorder patterns. If an item moves slowly, investigate whether the issue is price, placement, customer awareness, product fit or excessive initial stock. Avoid automatically increasing order quantities simply to secure a lower unit price.

8. Give each product a clear route to market

A product should have a defined target customer, value proposition and sales plan. Prepare accurate product information for sales teams, including category, pack size, relevant specifications and approved claims. Use supplier-provided material only where the distributor has permission and it complies with local rules.

Map target accounts and decide how the sales team will introduce the product. Ensure pricing, stock availability and fulfilment plans are aligned before making commitments to customers. Promotions and marketing activities must follow applicable alcohol advertising and responsible marketing requirements.

Measure results using sales volume, repeat orders, margin, stock turns and customer feedback. Review performance at a regular interval and make changes based on evidence rather than assumptions.

9. Questions to ask a prospective spirits supplier

Before adding products to the portfolio, ask:

  • Which products and pack sizes are currently available?
  • Are the products eligible for the intended territory, subject to required approvals?
  • What minimum order quantities, lead times and delivery terms apply?
  • Which product specifications and label documents can be provided?
  • What costs are included in the quotation, and what is excluded?
  • Are territory, brand or distribution arrangements relevant to this proposal?
  • How are product changes, shortages and quality concerns managed?
  • What information is needed to prepare a formal offer?

Record answers and conditions in writing. If the supplier needs a forecast or market plan to assess the request, provide realistic figures and identify assumptions.

10. Expand in stages and learn from results

A staged launch lets the distributor test demand while managing exposure. Begin with a defined assortment and customer group, subject to legal and commercial requirements. Set a review date and clear success measures before the products enter the market.

At review, compare actual sales and costs with the forecast. Assess availability, customer feedback, reorder frequency and the operational effort required. Expand only when there is evidence of demand and the supply model remains workable.

A portfolio is not static. Competitor activity, regulation, customer preferences and costs may change. Regular reviews help the distributor retain products that perform and reconsider those that do not.

Conclusion

  • A strong spirits portfolio is built around market fit, transparent landed cost, compliant operations, disciplined inventory and a supplier relationship that supports clear communication. Category breadth can be valuable, but only when the distributor can serve the market effectively.
