Choosing a Bottled Spirits Export Partner in India: A Practical Guide for Importers and Distributors

International buyers evaluating a bottled spirits supplier need more than a product list. They need to understand whether a supplier’s portfolio fits the target market, whether the commercial process is clear, and whether product, packaging and shipment requirements can be discussed early. For importers, wholesalers and distributors considering an Indian spirits partner, a structured evaluation helps reduce avoidable delays and supports a more informed buying decision.

Deejay Distilleries’ website presents a portfolio that includes whisky, vodka, gin, rum, brandy and exotic spirits, and describes export activity alongside domestic distribution. Buyers can use the company’s published product and export pages as a starting point for a direct commercial discussion. Product availability, destination-market eligibility, order quantities, lead times and terms should be confirmed with the company rather than assumed from a general catalogue.

1. Start with the needs of your market

Before approaching any supplier, define the market you serve. A national importer, a regional wholesaler, a hospitality-focused distributor and a retail-led business may require different formats, price points, category mixes and sales support. Build a short brief that answers:

  • Which country or territory will the products enter?
  • Which categories are relevant: whisky, vodka, gin, rum, brandy or other spirits?
  • What consumer segment and price band are you targeting?
  • Which bottle sizes and packaging formats are permitted and commercially practical?
  • What forecast volumes can you reasonably support?
  • Are you seeking established brands, a broader portfolio, or a discussion about a specific supply arrangement?

These questions help turn a general enquiry into a useful commercial conversation. They also make it easier for a supplier to identify which products merit further review.

2. Evaluate the portfolio, not just one product

A portfolio can create flexibility for a distributor, but breadth alone does not guarantee market fit. Review each relevant category separately. Consider how it aligns with local consumer preferences, competitor offerings, channel margins, shelf presentation and applicable product rules.

Deejay Distilleries’ public website lists multiple spirits categories and individual product pages. Use those pages to shortlist products for discussion, then ask for current specifications and destination-market information. Do not assume every listed product is available in every territory or that a package format shown online is automatically approved for your market.

A practical portfolio review should record the product name, category, proposed market, pack size under consideration, target channel, expected annual demand and any unanswered regulatory questions. This simple document makes comparison across suppliers more consistent.

3. Ask for product and quality documentation

A professional procurement process depends on verifiable product information. Depending on the product and jurisdiction, buyers may need specifications, alcohol-by-volume information, ingredient or composition details where applicable, packaging dimensions, batch identification information, certificates or test documents, and approved label artwork.

The exact documents vary by destination and product type. Ask the supplier to confirm which documents it can provide and which must be issued or approved by other parties. If your regulator or customs broker requires a particular format, share that requirement early.

Avoid relying on broad statements such as “international quality” without supporting details. Instead, agree on the specific product specification, testing expectations, release process and documentation required for each shipment. Any quality agreement should identify responsibilities, acceptance criteria, how discrepancies are handled and which version of the specification controls.

4. Confirm labels and market compliance before production

Alcoholic beverages are regulated differently across countries, and requirements may vary by category, alcohol strength, packaging, claims and route to market. Labels may need particular language, declarations, warnings, importer details, lot codes or other information. Taxes, excise rules, registration, permits and advertising restrictions may also apply.

The safest process is to have the importer, supplier and local compliance adviser review the destination market’s current rules before label artwork is finalised or a production commitment is made. In India, FSSAI publishes regulations for alcoholic beverages and subsequent amendments; these are useful reference points for applicable Indian requirements, but they do not replace the destination country’s rules.

Do not treat a label approved for one market as automatically suitable for another. Keep a written record of the approved artwork version and ensure the purchase order refers to it. Changes to language, bottle size, importer details or claims should be reviewed before printing.

5. Discuss packaging and logistics in detail

Packaging is both a brand presentation decision and a logistics consideration. Bottle size, case configuration, closures, labels, cartons, palletisation and protective materials can influence handling, storage, freight efficiency and damage risk. Confirm the exact pack configuration and whether it is suitable for the planned route.

The commercial discussion should cover the proposed shipment size, production and dispatch lead times, loading arrangements, transport responsibilities, insurance, destination handling and the documents expected to accompany the shipment. The agreed Incoterm, payment milestones and allocation of duties should be clearly stated in the contract or purchase order.

Avoid committing to a launch date until the critical path is understood. Label approval, product registration, production scheduling, packaging procurement, export documentation, freight booking and destination clearance may each affect timing. Ask for an indicative schedule and identify which milestones depend on buyer action.

6. Compare suppliers on a like-for-like basis

A low quoted unit price does not necessarily mean the lowest landed cost. Compare the same product specification, bottle size, case configuration, order quantity, delivery basis, payment terms and destination assumptions. Include freight, insurance, customs duties, excise, taxes, port charges, local distribution and any registration or compliance costs that apply.

Use a supplier scorecard covering product fit, documentation, responsiveness, specification clarity, packaging options, commercial transparency, expected lead time and issue-resolution process. Score only evidence you have received. Mark unknowns as unknown rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

A good supplier discussion should make uncertainty visible. If a price, quantity or lead time depends on final specifications, record that dependency. Clear qualification at the quotation stage can prevent disagreements after an order is placed.

7. Build a realistic launch plan

A first order is only one part of a sustainable distribution relationship. Prepare a launch plan that includes regulatory approvals, product master data, sales forecasts, channel selection, warehousing, pricing, trade marketing and reorder triggers. Define who owns each task and the date by which it must be completed.

For a new market, a phased launch may help the buyer learn which categories and formats perform best before expanding the assortment. Any phased approach should still respect local licensing, product registration and minimum order requirements. Do not assume that a smaller initial shipment is possible until the supplier and logistics partners confirm it.

After launch, review sales velocity, stock cover, product feedback, returns, breakage, forecast accuracy and reorder lead time. Share realistic forecasts with the supplier and revisit them when market conditions change.

8. Questions to ask an Indian spirits supplier

Before progressing to a commercial proposal, ask:

  • Which products and pack sizes are currently available for my target market?
  • What minimum order quantities and lead times apply?
  • Which product specifications and shipment documents can you provide?
  • What label or packaging customisation is possible, and what approvals are required?
  • Which party is responsible for registrations, label approval, export formalities and destination clearance?
  • What are the quotation validity, payment terms and delivery basis?
  • How are quality concerns, shipment discrepancies or damaged goods handled?
  • What information do you need from us to prepare a realistic proposal?

The answers should be recorded and confirmed in writing before an order is placed.
