Private Label and Custom Packaging for Spirits: Questions Buyers Should Resolve Early

For a spirits distributor, portfolio planning is a balance between customer demand, category coverage, working capital, local regulation and dependable replenishment.

Private Label and Custom Packaging for Spirits: Questions Buyers Should Resolve Early

Private-label or custom-packaged spirits projects require coordination across product specification, brand ownership, packaging supply, regulatory approval, production planning and commercial terms. A concept that looks simple at the design stage can become complicated if ownership, minimum quantities, label rules or packaging lead times have not been resolved.

Deejay Distilleries’ website mentions a range of spirits products and innovative packaging, but it does not establish that every private-label or bespoke packaging service is available. Buyers should therefore treat customisation as a topic for direct discussion and ask the company to confirm its capabilities, applicable restrictions and commercial conditions.

1. Define what “custom” means

Custom projects can refer to different levels of change: a new brand, a label adaptation, a different bottle or closure, a case-pack change, a market-specific label, or another packaging requirement. These are not interchangeable. Each can have different costs, lead times, tooling requirements and approval steps.

Prepare a brief that identifies the product category, target market, intended customer, desired pack size, proposed brand ownership and requested changes. Include a reference image only as inspiration, not as proof that the same bottle or packaging can be supplied. The supplier should assess feasibility and propose a specification.

Separate essential requirements from preferences. For example, a legally required label declaration is not optional, while a particular carton finish may be a design preference. This distinction helps the project team protect compliance and budget.

2. Validate the product concept and market fit

Before investing in artwork or packaging, define the commercial purpose of the product. Who is the intended buyer, which channel will sell it, and how does it differ from the alternatives already available? The answer should be based on market evidence, customer feedback and a realistic cost model.

Consider the expected retail or wholesale price, channel margin, competitive set, product category and territoryspecific rules. If the project serves multiple markets, map differences in language, labels, bottle formats and registrations before creating one universal design. A single artwork may not meet every market’s requirements.

The supplier can help clarify what product information is available, but the buyer should own the market strategy and provide a clear commercial brief. Do not assume that a custom design automatically creates a commercial advantage.

3. Agree on product specification and responsibility

The product specification should be separate from the visual design. It should identify the product, relevant measurable characteristics, packaging configuration, approved claims and applicable standards. The level of detail depends on the product and destination.

Define who approves the product specification, who signs off samples if required, and what process applies when the specification changes. If a brand owner expects a particular product profile, those expectations should be written as measurable requirements where possible and reviewed by appropriate technical personnel.

Avoid relying on terms such as “premium,” “smooth” or “international quality” as substitutes for a specification. Such descriptions may be useful in brand positioning, but they do not establish measurable acceptance criteria. Claims used on packaging or in promotion should be substantiated and lawful in the target market.

4. Review packaging feasibility and lead times

Packaging choices can affect cost, minimum quantities and production schedules. Bottle shape, glass colour, closure, label stock, printing method, carton design, inserts and case configuration may each have sourcing implications. Some components may require special procurement or tooling; others may be available from existing options. The supplier must confirm what is feasible for the specific project.

Ask for the available packaging specifications, sample process, tooling or setup charges if applicable, minimum order quantities, expected component lead times and replacement arrangements. Confirm who owns any custom tooling or artwork files and what happens if the relationship ends.

Do not finalise a launch date before packaging supply and approval dependencies are understood. A delay in one component can affect the entire schedule. A written timeline should show the buyer’s approval milestones as well as the supplier’s tasks.

5. Treat label compliance as a design requirement

Alcohol labelling rules vary by destination and product type. The label may need prescribed declarations, language, volume, alcohol content, importer information, warnings or other details. Certain claims, images or marketing phrases may be restricted. Requirements should be checked with qualified regulatory contacts before artwork is approved.

The buyer should provide destination-market requirements and identify the person authorised to approve the final label. The supplier should confirm whether the design is technically printable and whether it matches the agreed packaging specification. Keep a record of the approved version and prevent unapproved changes from reaching production.

Do not copy a competitor’s label or assume that its design is lawful for your product. Intellectual property, trademarks, geographic indications, packaging rights and misleading similarity should be reviewed as part of brand clearance. Obtain appropriate legal advice where necessary.

6. Establish brand ownership and usage rights

A custom project should state who owns the brand name, logo, artwork, product photography, packaging design and any commissioned assets. It should also clarify whether the supplier may use those assets for production only, for its own portfolio, or for other purposes. The answer should be recorded in the agreement.

Before ordering printed components, confirm that the buyer has the necessary rights to use all brand elements in the target markets. A designer’s delivery of a file does not necessarily transfer every usage right. Similarly, a supplier’s ability to print a logo does not establish trademark ownership.

Discuss whether the arrangement is exclusive or non-exclusive, and if exclusivity is proposed, define the territory, product scope, duration, performance obligations and exceptions. Never assume exclusivity unless it is expressly agreed in writing.

7. Build a transparent cost model

Custom projects can include more than the per-bottle product price. Costs may include artwork development, design adaptation, printing plates, moulds or tooling, packaging components, samples, testing, registration, freight and unused inventory. Which charges apply depends on the project.

Request an itemised quotation and identify one-time charges separately from recurring unit costs. Confirm the quantity assumptions, quote validity, payment milestones, taxes, delivery basis and treatment of unused printed materials if the order changes or is cancelled.

Model more than one order size where useful. A larger run may reduce a component’s unit cost but can also increase inventory exposure and working-capital requirements. Select an order size based on credible demand and the shelf life or obsolescence risk of packaging, not on unit price alone.

8. Define quality approval and change control

Agree how packaging and product conformity will be checked. Depending on the project, this may involve artwork proof approval, physical packaging samples, product samples where appropriate, inspection criteria and batch documentation. The buyer and supplier should decide which checks are required before production and which records accompany the shipment.

Set a clear process for changes. A new label, bottle, closure or supplier component can affect compliance, cost, fit or production timing. No change should be treated as accepted simply because it appears visually similar. Record the revised specification, approval date and commercial impact.

The agreement should also define how defects, shortages, transit damage or out-of-specification results are reported and resolved. This is easier to manage when acceptance criteria and responsibilities have been established in advance.

9. Plan the route to market before production

A finished product still needs an approved route to market. The buyer should confirm registrations, licences, importer responsibilities, taxes, distribution arrangements, warehousing and any restrictions on advertising or promotion. These requirements vary across jurisdictions and must be verified locally.

Build the route-to-market plan alongside the product brief. Identify the first customer group, expected launch volume, pricing, distribution coverage, storage arrangements and reorder trigger. Avoid producing large quantities of market-specific packaging before the buyer has validated demand and approvals.

For exports, coordinate the importer, supplier and logistics provider on documents, delivery terms, destination label rules and shipment timing. A product’s availability from a supplier does not itself establish that it can legally be sold in every market.

10. Questions to resolve before approaching a supplier

Prepare answers to these questions:

  • Which product category and target market are involved?
  • Is the request for an existing product, label adaptation or a more extensive custom project?
  • Who owns the brand and artwork?
  • Which pack sizes, packaging materials and case configuration are required?
  • What minimum quantities, setup costs and lead times apply?
  • What product and packaging specifications will govern acceptance?
  • Who pays for samples, tooling, testing and unused packaging?
  • What delivery terms and documents are required?
  • What is the realistic first-order forecast and replenishment plan?

A complete brief enables a supplier to identify gaps and discuss feasibility more efficiently. It also helps the buyer compare proposals consistently.
