Extra Neutral Alcohol (ENA): What Industrial Buyers Should Clarify Before Sourcing

Extra Neutral Alcohol (ENA) is a technical product category, so sourcing decisions should be based on specifications and intended use rather than a product name alone. Buyers in beverage manufacturing and other permitted applications need to confirm the required grade, applicable standards, documentation, logistics and legal permissions before agreeing on a supply arrangement.

Deejay Distilleries’ website states that the company undertakes production and export of Ethanol (ENA) and also supplies absolute alcohol described as perfume grade. This is a starting point for an enquiry, not a substitute for confirming current product specifications, availability, capacity, use restrictions or commercial terms directly with the company.

1. What does ENA mean?

In India’s Food Safety and Standards (Alcoholic Beverages) Regulations, 2018, “extra neutral alcohol” is included in the definition of neutral spirit or neutral alcohol. The regulations describe it as a product obtained through distillation and rectification after alcoholic fermentation of carbohydrates of agricultural origin, with a minimum alcoholic strength of 96% alcohol by volume. Buyers should consult the current consolidated regulations and amendments to determine the requirements that apply to their product and activity.

The term ENA should not be used as a complete technical specification. Two commercial offers may use the same term but still differ in the parameters, test methods, documentation or packaging that the buyer requires. The procurement brief should state the intended use and the precise specification needed.

2. Define the intended application first

A supplier can only assess a request properly when the buyer explains the intended application and jurisdiction. Requirements for an ingredient used in an alcoholic beverage may not be identical to those for alcohol intended for a fragrance-related application or another lawful industrial use.

Before requesting a quotation, document:

  • Intended application and end product.
  • Destination country and place of delivery.
  • Applicable legal or customer specification.
  • Required quantity and expected purchasing frequency.
  • Preferred packaging, storage and transport arrangements.
  • Testing, certificate and traceability requirements.
  • Any buyer-specific qualification or audit process.

Do not assume a grade described for one use is automatically suitable for another. Ask the supplier to confirm the product’s intended-use suitability in writing and have the buyer’s technical or regulatory team review it.

3. Create a written specification

A specification should describe measurable requirements and the methods used to evaluate them. Depending on the application, buyers may need to specify alcoholic strength, relevant impurity limits, appearance, sample handling, test methods, packaging and storage conditions. The actual list should be established with the technical team and applicable standards; it should not be copied blindly from another application.

For each parameter, identify the acceptable limit, measurement unit, test method and the document that will report the result. If a supplier uses a different test method, ask whether the results are comparable and whether the buyer accepts that method. Resolve differences before the first shipment.

Keep the specification version-controlled. If the buyer changes an acceptance limit or use case, review whether the change affects price, availability, compliance or qualification. The purchase order should refer to the agreed specification version rather than simply stating “ENA.”

4. Understand the role of documentation

Documentation supports product identification, regulatory review, quality assurance and shipment handling. Depending on the transaction, a buyer may request a technical specification, certificate of analysis, safety information, batch or lot details, invoice, packing list, transport documentation and export-related records. Not every document applies to every product or route, and the responsible parties can differ.

Ask the supplier which documents are available for the specific product and shipment, who issues each document, and when it will be provided. If a certificate must come from an independent laboratory or public authority, clarify that early. Do not describe a document as available until the responsible party has confirmed it.

For each batch, define how the certificate is linked to the delivered material. Agree on the sampling process, retention of samples where relevant, review timelines and the process for handling results outside the agreed specification.

5. Supplier qualification and quality controls

Supplier qualification is more effective when it examines a defined set of risks. Buyers should consider the supplier’s ability to provide the requested specification, document traceability, communicate changes and support the required shipment process. Depending on the application and risk level, a buyer may also need an audit, questionnaire, sample evaluation or technical review.

A practical qualification sequence is:

  • Share the intended use, jurisdiction and specification.
  • Ask the supplier to confirm whether the requirement can be considered.
  • Review available technical and regulatory documentation.
  • Evaluate a sample only where lawful, safe and appropriate.
  • Resolve differences in test methods and acceptance criteria.
  • Agree on the order, shipment and non-conformance process.
  • Reassess the supplier when the specification, process or applicable requirements change.

Avoid treating a supplier questionnaire as proof of compliance by itself. Verify the evidence that matters for the particular product and market.

6. Storage, transport and handling

Ethanol is flammable. Storage and transport arrangements must follow the relevant laws, site safety procedures, equipment requirements and transport rules. Buyers should involve competent safety, engineering and logistics personnel when determining suitable packaging, storage conditions, ventilation, ignition control, handling procedures and emergency measures.

The purchase specification and logistics plan should identify who is responsible for packaging, transport classification, safety documentation and carrier arrangements. Confirm compatibility between the selected packaging and the material, route and expected storage conditions. Never rely on a generic description of “industrial alcohol” to determine handling requirements.

If a shipment crosses borders, the importer and exporter should confirm applicable dangerous-goods rules, customs requirements and permits with qualified advisers and the relevant authorities. Requirements depend on the product, concentration, packaging and route.

7. Plan the commercial arrangement transparently

A useful quotation request gives the supplier enough information to assess the requirement without implying that price alone determines suitability. Include the target quantity, destination, required delivery basis, packaging, specification, documentation and desired schedule. Ask which assumptions are included in the offer and which costs or approvals remain outside it.

Compare quotations on the same basis. Unit price may not include freight, insurance, duties, taxes, handling, testing, permits or destination charges. Clarify quotation validity, payment milestones, order cancellation terms, shipment tolerances and how changes in quantity or timing are handled.

Do not assume minimum order quantities, production capacity or dispatch dates. Ask for confirmation for the specific requirement and obtain agreed terms in writing.

8. ENA, ethanol and absolute alcohol: avoid ambiguous requests

Buyers sometimes use “ethanol,” “ENA,” “neutral alcohol” and “absolute alcohol” interchangeably. This can create technical and commercial misunderstandings. Use the terminology stated in the applicable specification and explain the intended application. If the required product is described as absolute alcohol or perfume grade, request its exact specification and suitability documentation rather than assuming it is identical to ENA.

Deejay Distilleries’ website refers to both ENA and absolute alcohol for perfume-grade use. A prospective buyer should ask the company to confirm which product matches the buyer’s requirement, the current specification, applicable uses, documentation and destination restrictions. This article does not claim that every grade is available for every application.

9. Questions to include in an ENA enquiry

A concise technical enquiry should ask:

  • Can the requested product and specification be considered for the stated application?
  • Which specification and test methods are used?
  • What batch-specific quality documentation is available?
  • Which packaging and shipment arrangements can be discussed?
  • What order quantities, lead times and quotation assumptions apply?
  • Which regulatory approvals, licences or permits must be confirmed by the buyer?
  • How are samples, specification changes and non-conforming results handled?

The supplier’s response should be reviewed by procurement, quality, safety and regulatory teams as appropriate.

Conclusion

  • ENA procurement should begin with a clearly defined application and specification, followed by documentation review, supplier qualification, safe logistics planning and transparent commercial terms. A product label alone is not enough to establish suitability.
