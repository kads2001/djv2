Exporting Bottled Alcohol from India: A Buyer-Supplier Checklist for Market Readiness

Exporting bottled alcohol is a coordinated process involving the product supplier, importer, freight partners, customs professionals and the relevant regulatory authorities. A product that is commercially attractive may still be unsuitable for a market if the label, registration, documentation, tax treatment or import route has not been checked.

Deejay Distilleries describes export activity on its website and lists whisky, vodka, gin, rum, brandy and exotic spirits in its portfolio. Buyers should use the published pages to identify potential products for discussion, then confirm destination eligibility, current availability and commercial terms directly. This checklist is general information, not legal advice or a guarantee that a particular shipment can be exported or imported.

1. Confirm the product and destination before negotiating details

Start by defining the destination country, importer of record, intended sales channel and exact product category. Requirements may differ by country and sometimes by region or state. A shipment for one market should not be assumed suitable for another.

The buyer and supplier should agree on the product name, pack size, alcohol strength, packaging configuration and intended quantity. Confirm that the product is available for the proposed territory and that any relevant brand, registration or distribution arrangements have been reviewed. If the buyer needs a specific bottle format or label language, include it in the initial brief.

Create a shared product sheet with the commercial name, product category, pack size, specification version, destination and responsible contacts. This becomes the reference point for quotation, artwork, purchase order and shipment documentation.

2. Map the regulatory responsibilities

A cross-border alcohol shipment can involve several distinct responsibilities: export authorisation, product compliance, import permissions, excise and customs treatment, label approval, warehousing and onward distribution. Which party handles each requirement depends on the transaction and the applicable law.

Before ordering, identify the exporter, importer of record, local distributor and any licensed agent or broker required for the route. Ask each party to confirm its responsibilities in writing. Do not assume the exporter automatically obtains the destination-country approvals or that an importer’s general business licence covers every product category.

In India, FSSAI publishes alcoholic beverage regulations and amendments. Those materials should be checked for requirements relevant to the Indian side of the transaction. Destination-country regulators and qualified local advisers should be consulted for import-specific requirements. A general online checklist cannot replace a market-specific legal review.

3. Review labels before they are printed

Labelling is a common source of avoidable delays because artwork is often finalised too late. Requirements can cover product description, alcohol content, volume, language, responsible parties, origin, batch identification, warnings, ingredients or other declarations depending on the market and product.

The importer should provide current destination-market label requirements and have the proposed artwork reviewed by a qualified compliance contact. The supplier should confirm production feasibility and ensure the final approved artwork is the version used. Keep approval records, including version number and date.

Avoid making unverified claims on labels or marketing material. Claims relating to origin, ingredients, age, production method or quality should be accurate, supportable and permitted in the target market. If local rules require a particular statement or prohibit a claim, that requirement should be resolved before printing.

4. Assemble the shipment document plan

A shipment may require commercial and transport documents such as an invoice, packing list, transport document, origin documentation where applicable, product or batch quality records, permits and customs declarations. The exact set depends on the destination, product, trade terms and authorities involved.

Prepare a document matrix with these columns: document name, responsible party, required format, issuing authority, deadline, reviewer and status. Ask the customs broker and importer to confirm the list before dispatch. Some documents must be issued by an authorised body; others are prepared by the commercial parties. Do not assume that a template from a previous shipment is valid for a different destination.

Ensure the product descriptions, quantities, pack sizes, batch details and parties’ names are consistent across documents. Differences can create clearance questions or administrative rework. If an amendment is needed after issuance, confirm the correct correction process rather than informally editing a final document.

5. Confirm classification, duties and taxes

Tariff classification and tax treatment affect landed cost. The buyer and customs professional should confirm the applicable classification, customs duties, excise or alcohol-specific levies, value-added or sales taxes, port charges and other costs for the exact product and destination. Treatment may vary by country and product characteristics.

Do not estimate landed cost using a single generic tax rate. Build a calculation with separate line items and state the assumptions used. Confirm who pays each charge under the agreed delivery terms and who is responsible for customs clearance. The final price comparison should include all known costs to get the goods to the intended warehouse or point of sale.

If any cost remains uncertain, mark it as an estimate and identify the person responsible for confirming it. This makes the commercial decision more transparent and avoids presenting a provisional figure as guaranteed.

6. Agree on delivery terms and logistics

The contract or purchase order should identify the delivery basis, shipment point, destination, insurance responsibilities, loading arrangements and who manages export and import clearance. Incoterms may be useful when correctly selected and specified, but the parties should ensure the chosen term matches the actual route and responsibilities.

Plan the shipment backward from the desired arrival date. Include time for product confirmation, label approval, production, packaging, document preparation, freight booking, transit, customs clearance and delivery. Alcohol specific restrictions or carrier requirements may influence routing and handling. Confirm these with qualified logistics providers.

Packaging and case configuration should be agreed before production or dispatch. Consider handling, palletisation, breakage prevention, storage conditions and any destination requirements. The buyer should also confirm warehouse readiness and any local rules affecting receipt, bonded storage or release of the goods.

7. Use a purchase order that removes ambiguity

A clear purchase order should identify the product, specification, quantity, unit price, currency, packaging, approved artwork, delivery basis, schedule, payment terms and required documents. It should also state how the parties handle shortages, damaged goods, quality concerns, changes in schedule and disputes.

Ensure that the quotation and purchase order use the same product description and pack configuration. If the price depends on quantity, packaging or a particular delivery term, document the assumption. Confirm quotation validity and how exchange-rate or freight changes will be handled if relevant.

Avoid relying solely on informal messages for key terms. A written order confirmation helps both parties establish what was agreed and what remains conditional.

8. Prepare for shipment exceptions

Even a well-planned shipment can encounter delays. Common planning risks include missing approvals, inconsistent paperwork, incorrect labels, late freight bookings, unexpected destination charges or a mismatch between the ordered and delivered specification.

Before dispatch, hold a readiness review:

  • Is the product and destination confirmed?
  • Is the approved label version documented?
  • Are required permits and registrations in place?
  • Have the importer and broker reviewed the document set?
  • Do the shipment quantities and product details match across documents?
  • Are transport and insurance arrangements confirmed?
  • Is the receiving warehouse ready?
  • Are contact points available to resolve a customs or delivery question?

Agree on an escalation path and identify who can approve document corrections or schedule changes. A simple readiness check is often more useful than assuming all details will be resolved once the goods are moving.

9. Plan the post-arrival process

The work does not necessarily end when a shipment reaches the destination port. Customs release, excise or tax payment, inspection, local transport, warehouse receipt and product release may still be required. The importer should know which documents must be retained and which records support inventory and traceability obligations.

At receipt, check the delivered quantity, packaging condition, product identification and accompanying documents. Record any discrepancy promptly and follow the agreed contract process. Keep batch and shipment records in a form that supports future queries, recalls where applicable, tax records and reorder planning.

After the first shipment, review actual lead time, total landed cost, document accuracy, breakage or damage, and communication between the parties. Use the findings to improve the next order rather than repeating the same assumptions.

Conclusion: Treat export readiness as a shared responsibility
